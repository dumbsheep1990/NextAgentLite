## Abstract

The main objective of this study was to apply geopolymer (GP) particles as novelty fillers in natural rubber (NR) composites with various loadings $(0,{10},{20},{30}$ , and 40 phr). Influence of GP particles on cure characteristics, mechanical, dynamic, thermal, and morphological properties of the NR/geopolymer composites was investigated. It was found that incorporation of geopolymer particles significantly affected the cure characteristics and the mechanical properties. That is, faster curing was observed due to acceleration by metal oxide impurities. It was also found that ${100}\%$ modulus and tensile strength increased with the addition of ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ . This is attributed to the physical interactions of GP particles with NR molecules. In addition, the Payne effect and improved thermal stability were also observed in the GP-filled NR composites. It was concluded that ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ of GP in NR composites might be suitable for some industrial applications.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Polymer composites combine a polymer matrix with fillers. They are attractive in industrial applications and for academic research, due to control of the material properties. Natural rubber (NR) as a green and renewable polymer is widely used as a raw polymer material industrially, including in automotive parts and tires, because of its excellent mechanical and elastic properties. ${}^{\left\lbrack  1\right\rbrack  }$ However, fillers are necessary in most cases to modify the NR properties for specific applications. A wide variety of particulate fillers are used in the rubber industry for multiple purposes, including reinforcement, reduced material costs, and improved processing. ${}^{\left\lbrack  2\right\rbrack  }$ The most important fillers in rubber compounding are the conventional synthetic fillers based on carbon black and silica. They reinforce and enhance various properties such as tear strength, tensile strength, hardness, and abrasion resistance. ${}^{\left\lbrack  3\right\rbrack  }$ The incorporation of solid particles with large specific surface, such as synthetic silica, quartz, carbonaceous materials, and metal oxides, provided ample filler surface to interact with the rubber molecules, and hence these are very effective in improving rubber properties. ${}^{\left\lbrack  4\right\rbrack  }$ Synthetic silicas, for instance precipitated silica, possess siloxane and acidic silanol groups (Si-OH), ${}^{\left\lbrack  5\right\rbrack  }$ and have also been widely used in the polymer industries. ${}^{\left\lbrack  6\right\rbrack  }$ Typically the silica surfaces are hydrophilic, so they have strong tendency to adsorb moisture. ${}^{\left\lbrack  7\right\rbrack  }$ Furthermore, when large amounts of silica are added, the compound viscosity is increased substantially due to strong silica-silica interactions, ${}^{\left\lbrack  8\right\rbrack  }$ and the production of these conventional fillers is highly energy-consuming. Thus, alternative fillers from renewable resources are of considerable interest to improve the properties of rubber while improving sustainability and green aspects of the rubber life cycle.

In recent years, silica from natural sources has attracted attention as an alternative reinforcing filler in natural and synthetic rubber, because of low cost, good mechanical properties, dimensional stability, and environmental issues. ${}^{\left\lbrack  9\right\rbrack  }$ Types of natural fillers exploited in rubber composites include fly ash (FA), ${}^{\left\lbrack  {10}\right\rbrack  }$ rice husk ash (RHA), ${}^{\left\lbrack  {11}\right\rbrack  }$ oil palm ash (OPA), ${}^{\left\lbrack  {12}\right\rbrack  }$ bagasse fiber ash (BFA), ${}^{\left\lbrack  {13}\right\rbrack  }$ cellulose fiber, ${}^{\left\lbrack  {14}\right\rbrack  }$ coconut shell powder (CSP), ${}^{\left\lbrack  {15}\right\rbrack  }$ and lignin. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {16}\right\rbrack  }$ The FA is a byproduct from the combustion of pulverized coal in electric power generating plants. It is a potential alternative filler in rubber materials with nearly ${40} - {50}\mathrm{{wt}}\%$ silica content. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {10}\right\rbrack  }$ In addition, FA contains various chemical elements, including aluminum, iron, calcium, magnesium, sulfur, sodium, potassium, and carbon. On the other hand, RHA filler has also been tested in natural rubber compounds. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {17}\right\rbrack  }$ It was found that the incorporation of RHA significantly affected cure characteristics and mechanical properties of NR composites. That is, RHA caused faster curing and increased Young's modulus, tensile strength and other related strength properties. In addition, OPA filler was also used in rubber compounds, and tensile strength and elongation at break improved by about ${16}\%$ and ${7.4}\%$ , respectively. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {18}\right\rbrack  }$

Geopolymers (GPs) are polymerized chains or networks of inorganic aluminosilicate molecules linked with covalent bonds. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {19}\right\rbrack  }$ They are synthesized at room temperature from predominantly silica and aluminum materials of geological origin, or with by-products such as fly ash, as geopolymer-ization process shown in Figure 1. The geopolymers are a comparatively new type of material containing pozzolan components and alkaline solution. It is noted that fly ash has been frequently used as the pozzolan material in the synthesis of GPs. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {20}\right\rbrack  }$ Therefore, geopolymers can be produced from waste materials and thus reduce environmental problems. In particular, a new and green solution is to utilize fly ash to avoid its negative impacts on environment and ecology, instead trapping the toxic trace metal elements in geopolymer structures. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {21}\right\rbrack  }$ The fly ash-based geopolymers typically exhibit superior mechanical properties with low density and cage-like crystalline structure. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {19}\right\rbrack  }$ Furthermore, their durability has been recognized as nearly comparable to that of hydrated Portland cement, and they can be used as green cement. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {22}\right\rbrack  }$ Geopolymers might also have use as new alternative fillers that improve various properties of rubber composites. This is because geopolymers contain Si and Al ions that could form linkages with organic polymer molecules. Therefore, the geopolymer might interact with the rubber molecules, which enhances reinforcement effects. These might be innovative and advantageous fillers in natural rubber composites. Therefore, the novel NR/GP composites which were prepared in this work had benefit over the conventional fillers in particular low energy-consuming process.

In this work, novel geopolymer-filled NR composites were prepared with various geopolymer contents. The main objective was to determine the effects of geopolymer on the properties of NR composites. In addition, fly ash was also compounded with NR for comparison purposes. Cure characteristics, mechanical, dynamic, thermal, and morphological properties of geopolymer or fly ash-filled NR composites were investigated.

### 2.2 | Preparation of geopolymers

Fly ash was first mixed with ${10}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{{NaOH}}$ solution in a Hobart mixer for about $2\mathrm{\;{min}}$ , with a paddle speed of approximately ${285}\mathrm{{rpm}}$ . Then, the ${\mathrm{{Na}}}_{2}{\mathrm{{SiO}}}_{3}$ solution was added, and mixing was continued for another 2 min. The mixture was then immediately cast into a mold and conditioned at room temperature for at least $4\mathrm{\;{hr}}$ as the final setting time. It was then annealed by conditioning in a hot air oven at approximately ${65}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ for $4\mathrm{{hr}}$ and then further annealed in a microwave at ${450}\mathrm{\;W}$ for $5\mathrm{\;{min}}$ . Finally, the geopolymer product was ground in a ball mill for about ${48}\mathrm{{hr}}$ . Then, the geopolymer powder was sieved through a 200-mesh screen before compounding with NR. The functional groups in the geopolymer particles were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Vertex70, Bruker, Karlsruhe, Germany). Also, the particle size distribution of geopolymer was analyzed by a particle size analyzer (Beckman Coulter LS 230, Vernon Hills, IL, USA). The morphology of geopolymer particles was also imaged by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, FEI-Quanta 400, Jeol Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Furthermore, the density of geopolymer was gravimetrically measured, and its $\mathrm{{pH}}$ was determined by the procedure described in ISO 10523: 1994.

### 2.3 | Analysis of FA and GP

The chemical compositions of GP and FA were characterized by X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF, PW2400, Philips, Elisabeth, the Netherlands). Each powder was first dried to constant weight in a hot air oven at about ${100}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ over 14 hr. The sample was then treated in a muffle furnace by heating from 50 to $1,{000}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ . It was then mixed with a binder (i.e., WAX C) in the weight ratio 2:1 and then compressed to a thin sheet. Finally, the sheet samples were characterized by XRF.

### 2.4 | Preparation of NR/geopolymer composites

Natural rubber/geopolymer composites were prepared by dry mixing at room temperature using a two-roll mill. The compounding formulation and mixing schedule with ${13}\mathrm{\;{min}}$ total mixing time are shown in Table 2. It is noted that the compounds and vulcanizates with various geopolymer loadings (0,10,20,30, or40phr)are here labeled as gum NR, NR-GP10, NR-GP20, NR-GP30, and NR-GP40, respectively. The rubber compound was then sheeted out and conditioned at room temperature for ${12}\mathrm{{hr}}$ before vulcanizing and testing. It is noted that also neat fly ash was used to prepare FA/NR composites for a comparison purpose. The FA particles were prepared in a similar manner as the geopolymer, by ball milling for ${48}\mathrm{{hr}}$ .

### 2.5 | Cure characterization

Cure characteristics of the GP/NR and FA/NR composites were determined using a moving die rheometer (MDR) (Monsanto Co., Ltd., OH, USA). The measurements were performed at the fixed ${1.66}\mathrm{\;{Hz}}$ oscillation frequency with 1 arc degree amplitude, at ${160}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ . The scorch time (ts2), optimum cure time (tc90), and minimum $\left( {M}_{H}\right)$ and maximum $\left( {M}_{L}\right)$ torque were recorded. Then, the compounds were vulcanized in an electrically heated plate press, model PR1D-W400L450PM (Charon Tut Co., LTD., Bangkok, Thailand) under 70 bar compression pressure at ${160}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ . The GP/NR and FA/NR vulcanizate sheets were then cooled down to room temperature and conditioned for ${24}\mathrm{{hr}}$ . Dumbbell-shaped test specimens were eventually prepared by die cutting for further testing and characterization.

### 2.6 | Mechanical properties

The tensile properties of the GP/NR and FA/NR composites were determined using a universal tensile testing machine (Tinius Olsen, model 10ST, Salfords, England) with a crosshead speed of ${200}\mathrm{\;{mm}}/\mathrm{{min}}$ according to ISO 527. The dumbbell shape used was ISO 527 (type 5A). The stress-strain curves were recorded, and tensile strength and elongation at break were estimated from these curves.

### 2.7 | Dynamic properties

Dynamic properties in terms of storage modulus $\left( {\mathrm{G}}^{\prime }\right)$ were investigated using RPA 2000 (Alpha Technologies, Akron, USA). The strain sweep from 0.56% to 500% was performed at ${80}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ at the constant $1\mathrm{\;{Hz}}$ frequency.

### 2.8 | Thermal properties

Thermal stability of the GP/NR and FA/NR samples was characterized by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA; STA 6000, Perkin Elmer Inc., Norwalk, CT, USA). A sample of approximately ${20}\mathrm{{mg}}$ was placed in a ceramic pan and then heated at ${10}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{{min}}$ rate across the temperature range from 30 to ${600}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ , under nitrogen atmosphere.

### 3.2 | Geopolymerization and chemical composition of geopolymer and fly ash particles

Figure 1 shows the geopolymerization process based on the reaction of fly ash and alkaline solution. It can be seen that the alkali facilitated decomposition of aluminosilicate in the fly ash and then favored polycondensation. The reaction proceeds under mild temperatures in the range from 25 to ${90}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ , so it is considered energy efficient and practical. Apparently, the reactions between fly ash and alkali and condensation of the resultant ${\mathrm{{Si}}}^{4 + }$ and ${\mathrm{{Al}}}^{3 + }$ species took place, followed by nucleation, oligomerization, and polymerization. This finally led to new aluminosilicate-based polymers with amorphous three-dimensional geopolymer networks. The fly ash-based geopolymer paste was cast into a mold at room temperature to settle in its final form. The alkali activation of fly ash is critical to geopolymerization. It is known that the alkaline solutions (i.e., ${\mathrm{{Na}}}_{2}{\mathrm{{SiO}}}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{{NaOH}}$ ), silica, alumina, and aluminosilicate in fly ash, are important factors controlling geopolymerization and structure of the final geopolymer. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {27}\right\rbrack  }$ Upon hydrolysis, the -Si-O-Si- or -Si-O-Al- bonds in aluminosilicate were broken, and the active ${\mathrm{{Al}}}^{3 + }$ and ${\mathrm{{Si}}}^{4 + }$ species were released. These active species shared electrons with the $4{\mathrm{O}}^{2 - }$ ions to form nuclei and aluminosilicate oligomers of tetrahedral ${\mathrm{{SiO}}}_{4}$ and ${\mathrm{{AlO}}}_{4}$ . Then, the aluminosilicate oligomers could be linked to form polysialate with -Al-O-Si- chains, polysialate siloxo -Al-O-Si-Si- chains, and polysialate disiloxo -Al-O-Si-Si-Si-chains. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {28}\right\rbrack  }$ The geopolymer formed here consisted of ${30}\mathrm{{wt}}\%$ ${\mathrm{{SiO}}}_{2},{17.9}\mathrm{{wt}}\% \mathrm{{CaO}},{12.4}\mathrm{{wt}}\% {\mathrm{{Al}}}_{2}{\mathrm{O}}_{3}$ , and other substances (Table 4).

### 3.3 | Cure characteristics

Figure 5 shows the cure curves for the gum, and for the NR vulcanizates with various geopolymer loadings or with 10 phr fly ash. It is seen that the torque abruptly increased with the incorporation of ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ geopolymer from that of the gum compound. Furthermore, slight reversion (decreasing trend in torque after its maximum) was observed. This might be attributed to the degradation of di-and polysulfidic linkages that have poor thermal stability in the cross-linked system. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {29}\right\rbrack  }$ The reversion behavior became more pronounced with ${40}\mathrm{{phr}}$ of geopolymer due to changes in the cross-linked structure of the vulcanizate after the maximum vulcanization. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {30}\right\rbrack  }$ Furthermore, lower curing curves were observed with increasing GP loading. This might be due to the formation of larger geopolymer agglomerates with poor distribution in the NR matrix, which hindered the cross-linking reactions and weakened internal strength. Furthermore, it is also seen that the curing curve of the NR compound with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{FA}}$ was much below the curve for the compound with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ . This might be attributed to the larger particle size of fly ash (Figure 3), and it gives larger agglomerates with poorer distribution than the GP (Figures 6 and 7). Furthermore, the GP/NR (10 phr) vulcanizate exhibited higher delta torque than the gum NR vulcanizate or the NR/FA (10 phr). This indicates higher cross-link density in the composite with GP.

Table 6 summarizes the curing characteristics in terms of minimum, maximum, and delta torque, together with scorch and cure times for the gum NR, the NR/geopolymer, and the $\mathrm{{NR}}/\mathrm{{fly}}$ ash composites. It is noted that the minimum torque is proportional to the viscosity of the uncured compound, while the maximum torque is related to the extent of cross-linking and interactions in the rubber vulcanizate. It is clear that incorporation of geopolymer at ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ increased the maximum torque and torque difference by filler-polymer and filler-filler interactions that reduced the mobility of the rubber chains. It is noted that filler-polymer (geopoly-mer-NR) and filler-filler (geopolymer-geopolymer) interactions are physical or chemical interactions. In Table 6, it is also seen that scorch and cure times became shorter with increasing geopolymer loading. This might be due to the metal oxides $\left( {{\mathrm{{Al}}}_{2}{\mathrm{O}}_{3},{\mathrm{{Fe}}}_{2}{\mathrm{O}}_{3}}\right.$ , and $\mathrm{{MgO}}$ , see Table 4) as cure activators, accelerating the sulfur vulcanization of NR, ${}^{\left\lbrack  {10}\right\rbrack  }$ so that also the cure rate index increased with geopolymer loading. In addition, it is clearly seen that the cure rate index of the compound with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ was much lower than with 10 phr FA.

### 3.4 | Morphological properties

Figure 6 shows light microscopy images of gum together with GP- and FA-filled NR composites. It is seen that the gum NR vulcanizate had smoother fractured surfaces with smaller white $\mathrm{{ZnO}}$ particles. In contrast, the fractured surfaces of the NR/GP and NR/FA composites were rougher because of some geopolymer or fly ash agglomerates. The dispersion with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ in the $\mathrm{{NR}}$ composite was more homogeneous than that observed for the NR/FA (10 phr) composite. This might be attributed to the weaker filler-matrix interactions in NR/FA (Figure 6b) than in NR/GP composites (Figure 6c), as indicated by some voids or cavities in the light microscopy images of the former. In Figure 6(e, f), it is also seen that strong and large geopolymer agglomerates were clear in the NR-GP30 and NR-GP40 composites. This might be due to excessive filler loading and poor adhesion of GP particles with the NR matrix. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {10}\right\rbrack  }$ Higher magnification SEM micrographs are shown in Figure 7. Smooth surfaces with tiny white spots of $\mathrm{{ZnO}}$ were confirmed for the gum $\mathrm{{NR}}$ vulcanizate (Figure 7a). Furthermore, large GP agglomerates, cavities and voids around the GP aggregates are clear in composites with 30 or 40 phr GP loading (Figure 6e, f). Thus, it can be concluded that the optimum geopolymer loading in a NR composite might be in the range from 10 to ${20}\mathrm{{phr}}$ .

Figure 8 shows schematically the proposed GP and FA structures and their distribution in the NR matrix. The GP had more homogeneous dispersion with smaller agglomerates and less voids in the NR matrix (Figure 8a), and its larger specific surface caused more filler-rubber interactions increasing the curing torque (Figure 5) and improving the mechanical properties (Figure 9). Conversely, the FA gave larger agglomerates and more voids in the NR matrix (Figure 8b). The FA had higher contents of ${\mathrm{{Fe}}}_{2}{\mathrm{O}}_{3}$ and ${\mathrm{{Al}}}_{2}{\mathrm{O}}_{3}$ (Table 4) and larger particles (Figure 3), which might explain its tendency to agglomerate.

### 3.5 | Mechanical properties

Figure 9 shows the stress-strain curves for the gum, and the FA- and GP-filled NR vulcanizates with various geo-polymer loadings or with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ fly ash. It is seen that the NR composites with 10 or ${20}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ exhibited higher Young’s and ${100}\%$ moduli (i.e., final positions in the graph insert) and stress-strain curves raised above that of the gum vulcanizate. However, incorporating more than 20 phr GP (or 10 phr FA) gave similar stress-strain curves to the gum vulcanizate but with different failure location or elongation at break. The geopolymer at 10 or ${20}\mathrm{{phr}}$ reduced the mobility of molecular chains and hence increased stiffness of the material. In contrast, incorporating 30 or 40 phr GP (or 10 phr FA) lowered the ${100}\%$ modulus and the stress-strain curve from those of the gum NR vulcanizate. This might be due to agglomeration of filler (Figures 6 and 7) which reduced effective specific surface and reinforcing performance. This result agrees well with the high delta torque for the NR composite with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}\mathrm{{GP}}$ (Table 6).

The mechanical properties tensile strength and elongation at break, obtained from the failure points of the stress-strain curves in Figure 9, are plotted in Figure 10. It can be seen that the GP/NR (10 phr) vulcanizate exhibited higher tensile strength but lower elongation at break than the gum NR vulcanizate. This may be due to the interactions of geopolymer and NR that improved the strength but reduced the ability to extend. Furthermore, further increase in geopolymer loading decreased the tensile strength and the elongation at break. This again might be due to excessive filler loading creating flaws that initiate failure: voids, cavities, and filler aggregates (Figures 6 and 7). The lack of adhesion between rubber matrix and filler allowed formation of weak spots that degraded tensile strength and elongation at break. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {31}\right\rbrack  }$

### 3.6 | Dynamic properties

The storage modulus of a filled polymer composite is typically influenced by the interfacial interactions of inorganic filler particles with polymer matrix. Strain-induced filler-filler network breakdown typically occurs during dynamic mechanical experiments at a constant frequency and temperature. Figure 11 shows the storage modulus as a function of strain amplitude for the gum, and the FA- and GP-filled NR vulcanizates with various geopolymer loadings or with ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ fly ash, tested at ${80}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ and $1\mathrm{\;{Hz}}$ . Nearly linear viscoelastic behavior was observed for the gum NR vulcanizate, whereas nonlinear viscoelastic behavior was seen with both GP/NR and FA/NR composites. This might be attributed to the breakdown of filler-filler networks known as the Payne effect. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {32}\right\rbrack  }$ It is noted that ${\mathrm{G}}^{\prime }$ is a measure of the energy stored/released mechanically during cyclic deformations, and it decreased monotonically with the strain amplitude in all cases. Generally, the absolute value of ${\mathrm{G}}^{\prime }$ in the low-strain regime relates to filler-filler networks. It is seen that ${\mathrm{G}}^{\prime }$ in the low-strain regime for the GP-filled NR composites increased with filler loading, while the FA-filled NR composite had the lowest value. This implies that the filler-filler networks grew with geopolymer loading while the FA filler networks were weaker. It is noted that the network formation by a filler in NR matrix is strongly related to the specific surface area and the self-aggregation tendency. That is, the FA with lower specific surface than the GP had less interfacial filler-filler interactions.

### 3.7 | Thermal properties

Thermogravimetric analysis measures the amount and rate of mass change in a sample as functions of temperature or time in a controlled atmosphere. The measurement is used primarily to determine the thermal and oxidative stabilities of materials as well as their compositional properties. Figure 12 shows TGA and DTG thermograms of the gum NR, and the GP- or FA (10phr)-filled NR vulcanizates. The observed weight loss, residual mass at ${550}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ , and degradation temperature $\left( {T}_{\mathrm{d}}\right)$ are summarized in Table 7. It is clear that the thermal stability of GP-filled NR composites $\left( {{T}_{\mathrm{d}} = {376.5}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}}\right)$ was slightly improved from that of the gum $\mathrm{{NR}}\left( {{T}_{\mathrm{d}} = {375.7}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}}\right)$ by the filler-rubber interactions. Moreover, it is seen the NR/FA composites had the poorest thermal stability $\left( {{T}_{\mathrm{d}} = {372.8}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}}\right)$ , which might be attributed to the metal oxide impurities in FA (Table 4) that could facilitate thermal degradation of the polymer networks. ${}^{\left\lbrack  {11}\right\rbrack  }$ In Figure 12, it is seen that the first degradation step of the GP- or FA-filled NR composites started at about ${330}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ and was completed around ${450}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ , and in this temperature range, the degradation of natural rubber (polyisoprene) component is dominant giving the major peaks of the DTG curves. The second degradation stage occurred in the temperature range from 450 to ${600}^{ \circ  }\mathrm{C}$ , where the natural rubber component degrades in the presence of oxidized structures as well as by depletion of sulphidic cross-links. However, the decomposition temperatures at $5\% ,{10}\% ,{50}\%$ , and ${80}\%$ weight losses (Table 7) for the GP- or FA-filled NR composites were higher than corresponding temperatures for the gum NR vulcanizates. This might be due to the dispersed geopolymer and fly ash fillers enhancing the thermal stability of NR composites.

## 4 | CONCLUSIONS

Geopolymer (GP) was successfully used as an alternative novelty filler in NR composite vulcanizates. It was found that the GP-filled NR composites had faster scorch and cure times, and higher cure rate index than the gum NR vulcanizate. Furthermore, the NR composites with 10 phr GP exhibited higher ${100}\%$ modulus and tensile strength than the NR/FA composite with similar level of fly ash (FA) loading, due to the stronger interactions of GP and NR matrix and the better GP filler distribution with smaller aggregates. Furthermore, larger cavities or voids were observed in the composites with high GP contents (i.e., 30 and 40 phr), as excessive filler loading caused large agglomerates and cavities in the composites because the adhesion of NR and GP was not sufficiently strong. In addition, the NR composite with 10 phr GP exhibited finer and better dispersion of the particles in the NR matrix than seen in the NR/FA composite. It was also found that in the low-strain regime, the storage modulus ${\mathrm{G}}^{\prime }$ of NR composites increased with GP filler loading, while the FA-filled NR composite had the lowest value. This relates to the Payne effect and filler-filler interactions in the composites. In addition, the thermal stability of GP-filled NR composites was slightly improved from that of the gum NR. Therefore, it is concluded that that ${10}\mathrm{{phr}}$ of GP in NR composites might be suitable for some industrial applications.
